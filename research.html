Overview

My research lies at the interface of applied analysis and mathematical modeling. I study partial differential equation models of complex biological and porous media, with two main goals: establishing their well-posedness, and rigorously deriving effective macroscopic descriptions through homogenization and asymptotic limits.

A particular focus is on biphasic mixture and visco-morphoelastic models, which describe the mechanics of growing and remodelling biological tissues and tissue-engineering constructs.

Biphasic mixture models

Articular cartilage, hydrogels and tissue-engineering scaffolds are soft, hydrated materials: a porous, deformable solid matrix saturated by an interstitial fluid. Mixture theory treats both constituents as interpenetrating continua that occupy every point with volume fractions $\phi^s + \phi^f = 1$, each obeying its own balance laws and exchanging momentum through interphase drag. The flow of fluid through the deforming matrix is what produces creep, stress relaxation and load-driven nutrient transport in these tissues.

I investigate the mathematical modelling and analysis of biphasic mixture systems, focusing on the interaction between the cellular and fluid phases. My research addresses the well-posedness of the governing PDE systems and their homogenization, aiming to establish rigorous macroscopic descriptions of these complex biological materials.

Visco-morphoelastic systems

Living tissues grow, resorb and remodel while they deform. Morphoelasticity captures this by splitting the deformation gradient into a growth part and an elastic part, $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_e\,\mathbf{F}_g$, so that residual stresses appear naturally whenever growth is incompatible. Adding viscous effects gives visco-morphoelastic models for processes such as wound healing, tumour growth and soft-tissue remodelling.

My current work analyses an incompressible visco-morphoelastic system in Eulerian form: the existence of weak solutions and the vanishing-diffusion limit. Objective (frame-indifferent) stress rates such as the Jaumann derivative play a central role. See the preprint on arXiv:2610.01487.

Homogenization

Biological tissues and porous materials are heterogeneous on a microscopic length scale $\varepsilon$ that is far smaller than the size of the sample or organ, so resolving the microstructure directly is impractical. Homogenization theory replaces the rapidly oscillating microscopic problem with an effective macroscopic one as $\varepsilon \to 0$, and justifies the limit rigorously through two-scale convergence and periodic unfolding. Classical examples include Darcy's law derived from Stokes flow in a periodic pore space, and Biot's poroelasticity derived from fluid–structure interaction.

I use these tools to pass from microscale biphasic and growth models to effective macroscopic equations, and to understand how the effective coefficients depend on the microstructure.
